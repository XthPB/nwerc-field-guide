Optimizing transitions

Two choices of predecessor would cost 2x+1 and 7−x at a destination x. Which choice gives the cheaper transition for each destination? Start by comparing them at x = 0, 2 and 4 before interpreting the crossing.

Start this topic in reading order → · See the whole reading path

  • Evaluate a moving segment cost instead of recomputing itA contiguous piece of a sequence costs one for each unordered pair of equal entries inside it. You must evaluate many candidate pieces with nearby endpoints while choosing cuts. For example, aba contains one equal pair, and appending one more a creates two new pairs.
  • Why the best partition cut moves to the rightSplit a nonnegative array into a fixed number of nonempty contiguous groups. A group costs the square of its sum, and you want the smallest total cost. Computing the best final cut separately for every endpoint and group count can be too slow.
  • Bound the split between two shorter intervalsYou have nonnegative pile weights in a row. A move merges two neighboring piles and costs their combined weight. Find the minimum total cost to merge the whole row. Many overlapping intervals need almost the same search for their final merge.
  • A totally monotone transition matrixYou need the cheapest column in each row of a large table, and you can ask for the cost of any cell. There is a promise about every subtable you can get by deleting rows and columns. Going down its rows, the leftmost cheapest column can stay put or move right. How many cells must you actually inspect?
  • Turn each predecessor into a lineYou may jump forward through a sequence of platforms with known heights. Each jump costs a fixed fee plus the square of the difference between its endpoint heights. Starting at the first platform, find the minimum total cost to reach each later one.
  • An envelope with arbitrary insertions or query orderA stream of earlier choices offers cost formulas of the form m·x+b. Formulas arrive in arbitrary order, and each query supplies a value of x. Return the smallest cost among all formulas available so far. A choice that loses at one x may win at another.
  • Optimize a ratio by subtracting a trial priceChoose exactly two items. Each has a benefit and a positive time, and your score is the combined benefit divided by the combined time. With pairs (6,2), (4,1) and (7,4), find the highest score. Averaging the two individual ratios would give a different objective.
  • Trade a prescribed count for a penaltyYou must split an input into exactly two groups at minimum cost. A faster subroutine can optimize without the exact-count requirement if you charge a fee per group. Can you recover the required answer from that subroutine? Test it when the best costs for one, two and three groups are 9, 5 and 3.
  • Carry a convex piecewise-linear cost functionYou may change each entry of 5, 1, 2 to an integer of your choice. Changing a to b costs |a−b|. Make the final sequence nondecreasing while minimizing the total change. The possible new values can span a very large range.

Test your recognition
Squared segment sums gave monotone split indices on nonnegative input. Can you reuse that proof when negative values enter?

The four-term proof contains a product of two prefix increments. With mixed signs it can be negative. Return to the baseline or prove a different property.

You priced each selected group and the optimal count skipped 6. May you immediately subtract the price times 6?

Only with a proved supporting segment at 6, such as discrete convexity on feasible counts and an appropriate tie penalty. Monotonicity of the chosen count by itself is insufficient.
