‹ Interval and partition DP

Cut a circular instance at a justified boundary

An operation may combine neighbouring pieces of a circular necklace, including its first and last entries. How can an interval DP see that join?

Work through the choice

Write the necklace twice. Every arc of at most one turn is now contiguous somewhere. Select a boundary justified by the final operation or try all rotations. When counting, decide whether two rotations represent one answer.

The rule you can reuse

Doubling the sequence makes each circular arc contiguous. If the answer can be cut at a boundary that survives to the final step, evaluate all length-n windows. A fixed cut is legal only if every optimal solution can be represented there.

For circular adjacent merging, compute linear interval costs on a+a for lengths ≤n, then minimize D[i,i+n−1] over 0≤i<n.

Check the boundary. For counting circular arrangements the same object can appear under many cuts. Anchor a labeled element, a unique extremum, or correct symmetry with a proved counting argument.

Cost O(n³) with ordinary split transitions and O(n²) memory. Doubling adds a constant to the dimension, not a free extra cut loop.

From idea to code

  • Interval DP in increasing length
    Use the increasing-length loop for two smaller intervals. Supply the base cases, valid split positions, immediate cost and choice of min, max or sum from the problem.

An interval records membership for free through its two endpoints. For an arbitrary small set, make membership explicit with a bitmask.
