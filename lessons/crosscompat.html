‹ Meet in the middle

When the two halves interact through conflicts

You split a set of incompatible guests into two halves. Which information from the left determines whom you may still invite on the right?

Work through the choice

If guest a forbids x while b forbids y, equal left group sizes allow different right completions. Summarize a left group by the mask it forbids. Query the best valid right subset of the complementary mask.

The rule you can reuse

If each object conflicts with a few others, sums no longer characterize compatibility. For each left mask compute which right vertices remain allowed. Precompute the best independent subset inside every right mask.

D(M)=max⁡{D(M∖{v}), wv+D(M∖({v}∪N(v)))}D(M)=\max\{D(M\setminus\{v\}),\,w_v+D(M\setminus(\{v\}\cup N(v)))\}D(M)=max{D(M∖{v}),wv​+D(M∖({v}∪N(v)))}

best[M]=max(best[M without v], value(v)+best[M without v and its neighbours]). Query best[allowedR] for each independent left subset.

Check the boundary. The two halves must be disjoint. Check conflicts within the left half, within the right half, and across the cut. An arbitrary cross-interaction may need an exponentially large interface again.

Cost O(2ʰ+2ⁿ⁻ʰ) word operations with single-word adjacency masks and low-bit recurrences, plus output/reconstruction costs.

From idea to code

  • Enumerate submasks
    The loop visits every nonempty submask. Add the empty option separately if it is legal. Enumerating submasks for every mask costs three to the power n across all masks.
  • Sum over submasks
    The per-bit sweep aggregates values from subsets. Choosing a representative or a maximum needs its own combining operation and identity.
