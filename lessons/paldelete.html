‹ Interval and partition DP

Let matching endpoints join a palindrome deletion

One move erases any consecutive palindrome in the current sequence. How can A, B, C, A vanish in two moves even though the original sequence reads differently backwards?

Work through the choice

Erase B first. A, C, A is now a palindrome and goes in one move. More generally, consider the move that removes the left endpoint. It can go alone or together with an equal rightmost partner in that move. If there are elements between them, perform a best deletion plan for the middle and wrap its last palindrome in these equal endpoints. The suffix beyond the partner is an independent deletion problem.

The rule you can reuse

D(l,r)=min⁡ ⁣(1+D(l+1,r), min⁡l<k≤ral=ak[max⁡(1,D(l+1,k−1))+D(k+1,r)])D(l,r)=\min\!\left(1+D(l+1,r),\ \min_{\substack{l<k\le r\\a_l=a_k}}\left[\max(1,D(l+1,k-1))+D(k+1,r)\right]\right)D(l,r)=min(1+D(l+1,r), l<k≤ral​=ak​​min​[max(1,D(l+1,k−1))+D(k+1,r)])

Use zero for an empty interval. Before the endpoints disappear, their presence keeps the middle separate from the suffix. On the removal turn any middle survivors form a palindrome too, which proves the matched case cannot cost less than the middle plan. The split tries every possible partner.

Check the boundary. A game that permits only a single colour per erased block gives a different answer on A B A. It requires two moves there. When scoring depends on how many equal pieces are bundled, the interval may need a third carry coordinate.

Cost O(n³) time and O(n²) memory for the displayed recurrence.
