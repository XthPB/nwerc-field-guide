Pass one message up a tree · Lesson 90 of 235

At most one child can take a special edge

You’re given a tree. You may mark an edge if neither of its endpoints touches any other marked edge. Find the maximum number of edges you can mark. At a branch, several children can compete for the same parent.

1. Work one example

At a vertex start with the best total when it matches no child. Matching one child changes that child's contribution by a calculable gain. Choose the best single gain because all child edges share their parent.

2. What do we need to remember?

A vertex can match at most one child. First total what the children achieve without it, then test the gain from each candidate match.

3. How do we get the next answer?

For maximum matching in a tree, decide whether u matches a child. Start from the sum of each child’s independent optimum. Forcing an edge u–v replaces v’s optimum with its value when v cannot use any child edge.

Fu=∑vBv,Bu=Fu+max⁡(0,max⁡v{1+Fv−Bv})F_u=\sum_v B_v,\qquad B_u=F_u+\max(0,\max_v\{1+F_v-B_v\})Fu​=v∑​Bv​,Bu​=Fu​+max(0,vmax​{1+Fv​−Bv​})

free[u]=Σ best[v]; best[u]=free[u]+max(0, maxᵥ(1+free[v]−best[v])). Here free[u] reserves u for its parent.

Check the boundary. For degree quota b use the largest allowed positive gains, taking account of whether a parent edge already consumes a slot. Pairwise child interactions may defeat independent gains.

Cost O(n) for one gain, O(Σ deg(u) log deg(u)) if sorting gains for general quotas.

Try one change

Taking child edge A gives an improvement of 5 and child edge B gives 4. Can a matching collect both improvements?

See the reasoning

No, both edges meet the same vertex. Add the better eligible gain to the baseline where neither edge is taken.
