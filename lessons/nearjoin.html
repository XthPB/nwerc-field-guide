Cut the search in half · Lesson 70 of 235

Closest, at most, or inside an interval

One half already contributes 2 and the target is 6. The other half can contribute 0, 3, 5 or 8. How near can you get?

1. Work one example

The ideal partner is 4. In a sorted list its closest neighbour must be the first number at least 4 or the one just before it. A target interval instead gives two search boundaries.

2. What do we need to remember?

A left sum gives a target for the other half. In sorted right sums the best attainable neighbor is close to its insertion point.

3. How do we get the next answer?

Keep the same half-sums and change the join operation. For closest, minimize distance to T−x. For counts in a range use ranks. For largest sum under a cap, find the last permissible right sum.

N([A,B])=∑x∈L(#{y∈R:y≤B−x}−#{y∈R:y<A−x})N([A,B])=\sum_{x\in L}\left(\#\{y\in R:y\le B-x\}-\#\{y\in R:y<A-x\}\right)N([A,B])=x∈L∑​(#{y∈R:y≤B−x}−#{y∈R:y<A−x})

Count [A,B] by Σₓ (upper_bound(R,B−x) − lower_bound(R,A−x)). For closest inspect lower_bound(R,T−x) and its predecessor when present.

Check the boundary. Handle empty-end iterators and tie policies. One nearest value suffices to optimize distance; counting all nearest subsets requires multiplicities and both sides of a tie.

Cost O(|R| log |R| + |L| log |R|). For a single cumulative bound with both lists sorted a pointer sweep costs O(|L|+|R|) after sorting.

Try one change

You need a right sum near 8 and the sorted choices are 3, 7 and 12. Which two need comparison?

See the reasoning

Check 7 and 12, the neighbors around 8. For an upper bound of 8 instead, only 7 is feasible among them.
