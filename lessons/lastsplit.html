‹ Interval and partition DP

Split on the final merge, pivot, or triangle

Merge neighbouring piles 3, 1 and 2 until one remains. Each merge costs the weight you combine. Which order costs less?

Work through the choice

Look backwards at the final merge. Its cost is always 6. Immediately before it the piles formed two contiguous groups, so choose a boundary and solve both sides independently.

The rule you can reuse

When the first move changes future adjacencies, reason backward from the final move. An interval can split into two independent subintervals once its final split is known. Process intervals in increasing length.

D(l,r)=min⁡l≤k<r{D(l,k)+D(k+1,r)+C(l,k,r)}D(l,r)=\min_{l\le k<r}\{D(l,k)+D(k+1,r)+C(l,k,r)\}D(l,r)=l≤k<rmin​{D(l,k)+D(k+1,r)+C(l,k,r)}

D[l,r] = minₗ≤k<r (D[l,k]+D[k+1,r]+cost(l,k,r)). A singleton has merge cost zero.

Check the boundary. For polygon triangulation, keeping chord (l,r) fixes a triangle (l,k,r). In a nonconvex polygon verify visibility and validity of each split. Matrix-chain costs depend on k and generally fail Knuth’s split conditions.

Cost O(n³) time, O(n²) memory before any proved acceleration.

From idea to code

  • Interval DP in increasing length
    Use the increasing-length loop for two smaller intervals. Supply the base cases, valid split positions, immediate cost and choice of min, max or sum from the problem.
  • Knuth-optimized adjacent merging
    For nonnegative pile weights this interval cost is their sum. Preserve consistent tie breaking when bounding a split by its two neighbours.
