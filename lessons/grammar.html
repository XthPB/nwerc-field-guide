Noncrossing pairs, parsing, and interleaving

Place four ordered points on a line. Pair all points and draw one arc above the line for each pair. Arcs may nest but may never cross. Count the possible complete pairings. Pairing the first point creates separate obligations inside and outside its arc.

1. Work one example

Fix the first point's partner. Any point inside that arc must pair on the inside, because an edge to the outside would cross. Multiply the numbers of independent inside and outside pairings, then sum over partners.

2. What do we need to remember?

A substring can derive a grammar symbol by splitting into two substrings deriving the required children. Sum or test all legal splits.

3. How do we get the next answer?

A noncrossing matching splits cleanly at the partner of its first vertex. In a grammar, a root production splits a substring into two parts. Count independent subobjects by multiplication and distinct root choices by addition.

F(l,r)=∑legal partners kF(l+1,k−1)F(k+1,r)F(l,r)=\sum_{\text{legal partners }k}F(l+1,k-1)F(k+1,r)F(l,r)=legal partners k∑​F(l+1,k−1)F(k+1,r)

For perfect noncrossing pairs, F[l,r]=Σ legal k F[l+1,k−1]F[k+1,r], with F[empty]=1. In a binary grammar add the nonterminal to the cell.

Check the boundary. When counting chronological orders of independent moves, multiply also by a binomial interleaving factor. When counting final structures, that factor would overcount.

Cost O(n³ × relevant rules) for a direct binary parse. For pure homogeneous pairing the length-only Catalan recurrence is smaller.
