‹ Knapsack and subset sum

Choose objects once or many times · Lesson 15 of 235

When do all subset sums form one interval?

You have coins with positive integer values, and each coin can be spent once. Find the smallest positive amount that no selection can pay exactly. For coins worth 1, 2, 2 and 7, the two coins worth 2 are separate pieces.

1. Work one example

One coin of value 1 covers 0 and 1. Taking the next coin of value 2 adds 2 to each of them, giving 2 and 3. The two ranges touch. After the second 2, all totals through 5 are possible. The next coin is 7, so 6 is missing.

2. What do we need to remember?

Keep one number R only while you have proved that every total from zero through R is reachable.

3. How do we get the next answer?

[0,R] ∪ [w,w+R]=[0,R+w]when w≤R+1.[0,R]\ \cup\ [w,w+R]=[0,R+w]\quad\text{when }w\le R+1.[0,R] ∪ [w,w+R]=[0,R+w]when w≤R+1.

Sort positive coins. Skipping w gives the old interval, taking w gives the shifted interval. They overlap or touch exactly when w ≤ R+1. For c equal copies satisfying this test, repeated shifts extend coverage to R+cw. In a construction where a new entry must first be made from earlier coins, use the stronger test w ≤ R before adding it.

Check the boundary. The bag 1,3 makes 0,1,3,4. Storing just its minimum and maximum invents total 2. The proof is about contiguous reachability, not just the extrema.

Cost O(n log n) time for n denominations and O(1) additional scan memory after sorting. A general Boolean bitset remains useful when holes survive.

Try one change

All totals from 0 to 4 are reachable. What changes if the next coin is 5? What if it is 6?

See the reasoning

Adding 5 gives totals 5 through 9, touching the old interval. Adding 6 starts at 6 and leaves 5 missing. If the coin itself must already be buildable, even 5 fails.
