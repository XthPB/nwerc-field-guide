‹ Digit DP, binary bounds and carries

Follow tight bounds digit by digit · Lesson 106 of 235

Attach a residue, digit mask, position, or automaton

Count the positive integers in a range that are divisible by every nonzero digit appearing in their own decimal representation. For instance, a number containing both 2 and 3 must pass both divisibility tests. A digit 0 adds no divisibility requirement.

1. Work one example

All observed digits divide a small common multiple. Remember their least common multiple and the number's remainder modulo a fixed multiple such as 2520. At the end, check whether the completed number is divisible by the recorded requirement.

2. What do we need to remember?

Combine a numerical-bound state with a small state for the property you test, such as a remainder or the LCM of digits already seen.

3. How do we get the next answer?

Build the product of the bound machine and the property machine. Keep only the property values that affect future acceptance. Digit divisibility sometimes reduces a set of previously seen nonzero digits to their LCM.

r′=(br+d) mod m,ℓ′=lcm⁡(ℓ,d)(d≠0)r'=(br+d)\bmod m,\qquad \ell'=\operatorname{lcm}(\ell,d)\quad(d\ne0)r′=(br+d)modm,ℓ′=lcm(ℓ,d)(d=0)

Remainder r′=(base·r+d) mod m. For divisibility by every nonzero decimal digit seen, carry ℓ=lcm(seen) and the number modulo 2520; at the end require r mod ℓ=0.

Check the boundary. 2520 is lcm(1…9). Do not take an LCM with zero in this variant. If constraints depend on position in the ordinary representation, count real digits or process fixed lengths separately.

Cost O(D·base·#LCM states·2520) for the decimal example. Sparse states, divisors-only indexing and free-suffix precomputation reduce constants.

Why can two prefixes with remainder 2 modulo 7 be grouped when testing divisibility by 7?

Appending d sends either remainder to (10×2+d) modulo 7. Keep any bound or digit-rule flags that can still distinguish the prefixes.
