Count paths without visiting every cell · Lesson 182 of 235

Sparse obstacles and a first-bad-event subtraction

You must cross a very large grid from its upper-left corner to its lower-right corner, moving one cell right or down each time. Only a short list of cells is blocked. Count valid paths when the full grid is too large to store.

1. Work one example

Count all unrestricted routes first. Assign each invalid route to its first obstacle. For the centre of a 3 by 3 board, two prefixes and two suffixes give four invalid routes out of six.

2. What do we need to remember?

Only the few obstacles can break an unrestricted path count. Subtract paths according to the first obstacle they hit.

3. How do we get the next answer?

With huge dimensions and few blocked points, count unconstrained monotone paths by binomial coefficients, then subtract those whose first obstacle is known. Include the destination after the obstacle list.

Fi=P(s,i)−∑j≺iFj P(j,i)F_i=P(s,i)-\sum_{j\prec i}F_j\,P(j,i)Fi​=P(s,i)−j≺i∑​Fj​P(j,i)

F[i]=paths(start,i)−Σ earlier dominated obstacles j F[j]·paths(j,i). Dominance here means both coordinates of j are ≤ those of i.

Check the boundary. Sort by coordinates, deduplicate obstacles, and handle blocked start or goal. Factorial/inverse-factorial binomials modulo p require a suitable prime and factorial range below p for the basic formula.

Cost O(K²) binomial queries for K obstacles plus preprocessing. Succeeds when K is small even if HW is enormous.

Try one change

For one blocked cell on a monotone grid, how many source-to-target paths pass through it?

See the reasoning

Multiply the number of paths to the cell by the number from it to the target. With several obstacles, charge each bad path to its first obstacle.
