‹ Probability, expectations and games

Measure a random process · Lesson 126 of 235

Count interchangeable species and condition on a real change

In a population of three competing species, two animals are picked at random. How likely is each species to survive?

1. Work one example

Individuals of one species are interchangeable, so store only the three counts. Some picks cause no change. Condition on a pair that does change the state and use the relative numbers of cross-species pairs as weights.

2. What do we need to remember?

When objects with the same occupancy behave identically, count how many objects are in each occupancy class.

3. How do we get the next answer?

A process on many labeled individuals may depend only on population counts. Two microstates may share one cell if the sum of transition probabilities into every next count class agrees. Encounters with equal species that leave the counts unchanged can be removed when computing the eventual winner.

Z=rs+sp+pr,P(scissors lose∣change)=rsZZ=rs+sp+pr,\qquad P(\text{scissors lose}\mid\text{change})=\frac{rs}{Z}Z=rs+sp+pr,P(scissors lose∣change)=Zrs​

For rock, scissors, paper counts (r,s,p), let Z=rs+sp+pr. Conditional on a population change, scissors lose with probability rs/Z, paper with sp/Z and rocks with pr/Z. Every such transition decreases r+s+p.

Check the boundary. At Z=0 the state is already absorbing. Ignoring self-loops preserves final-winner probabilities but changes expected time. To count all pair draws, charge total unordered pairs divided by Z per change when pairs are sampled uniformly without replacement.

Cost O((R+1)(S+1)(P+1)) population states, three outgoing changes each. Push mass in decreasing total, or memoize the three winner probabilities from each state.
