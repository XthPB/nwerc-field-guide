Reason about random choices · Lesson 214 of 235

Replace a random elimination order with random priorities

A uniformly random elimination order removes a vertex and its descendants. How often does a vertex get its own turn?

1. Work one example

Give each vertex a distinct random priority. A vertex gets a turn exactly when its priority comes first among itself and its ancestors. Add those probabilities using linearity of expectation.

2. What do we need to remember?

Give each item a random priority once. The induced elimination order can expose a much smaller probability calculation.

3. How do we get the next answer?

Imagine choosing a uniformly random surviving tree vertex and removing its entire rooted subtree. Equivalently scan one random permutation and ignore vertices already gone. A vertex earns a turn exactly when it appears before all its strict ancestors.

E[T]=∑vPr⁡[v is selected]=∑v1depth(v)+1\mathbb E[T]=\sum_v\Pr[v\text{ is selected}]=\sum_v\frac{1}{\mathrm{depth}(v)+1}E[T]=v∑​Pr[v is selected]=v∑​depth(v)+11​

If depth(root)=0, P(v is chosen)=1/(depth(v)+1). Linearity gives E[number of turns]=Σᵥ1/(depth(v)+1). Compute depths once instead of storing a distribution over surviving forests.

Check the boundary. The permutation coupling works because the first still-eligible element of a uniform remaining order is uniform over eligible vertices. Biased choices or recovery of removed vertices can break this coupling.

Cost O(n) after reading the tree. More generally, first among d exchangeable competitors has probability 1/d. Specify exactly which competitors can stop the event.

Try one change

A vertex has two ancestors. In a uniformly random elimination order, when does the vertex get its own turn?

See the reasoning

When it is first among itself and its two ancestors. Each is equally likely to be first, giving probability one third.
