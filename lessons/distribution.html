‹ Probability, expectations and games

Measure a random process · Lesson 119 of 235

Follow probability through one trial at a time

Two independent coins show heads with probabilities 1/2 and 3/4. Flip each once. Find the probability of seeing exactly one head, counting either order of heads and tails. With many coins you may need the complete distribution of head counts.

1. Work one example

Keep a probability for each possible number of heads. When tossing a coin, split every old probability between staying at the same count and increasing it by one. The two ways to get one head add.

2. What do we need to remember?

At each step move probability mass from old outcomes to their possible successors. Check that the total mass stays correct.

3. How do we get the next answer?

Store mass by the statistic needed at the end, then condition on the next independent event. If only a mean of a sum is requested, linearity can eliminate the distribution completely.

For head probability p, new[k]=(1−p)old[k]+p·old[k−1], with old[0]=1 initially.

Check the boundary. Do not multiply marginals when trials are dependent. Enlarge the state to include the conditioning information such as inventory remaining.

Cost O(n²) for all head counts up to n, O(n) memory. Some independent identical probabilities permit a direct binomial calculation.
