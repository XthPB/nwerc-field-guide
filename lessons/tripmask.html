‹ Geometry inside a DP

Geometry can determine the price of a combinatorial move

A courier can collect one or two points per round trip. With squared-distance prices, when is a paired trip cheaper?

Work through the choice

For a pending set, anchor its first point. Either fetch it alone or pair it with one other pending point. Geometry computes the price of that whole trip and a smaller mask handles the rest.

The rule you can reuse

When every trip starts and ends at a depot and may serve only one or two points, past trip order has no effect on later ones. Precompute each trip price. The remaining state is the set of unserved points.

D(S)=min⁡P⊆S, min⁡S∈P1≤∣P∣≤2{trip(P)+D(S∖P)}D(S)=\min_{\substack{P\subseteq S,\ \min S\in P\\1\le |P|\le2}}\{\mathrm{trip}(P)+D(S\setminus P)\}D(S)=P⊆S, minS∈P1≤∣P∣≤2​min​{trip(P)+D(S∖P)}

Pick the lowest unserved i. Either serve i alone, or pair it with one other unserved j. For squared Euclidean leg cost the paired price is d(O,i)²+d(i,j)²+d(j,O)². Record the minimizing partner.

Check the boundary. Fixing i eliminates permutations of trips. If the agent remains at the last customer, the future also needs its position. Asymmetric travel requires comparing both orders of a pair.

Cost O(n2ⁿ) time and O(2ⁿ) memory. At n=24 one int32 per mask is 64 MiB. Widen coordinate differences before squaring if bounds require it.

From idea to code

  • Enumerate submasks
    The loop visits every nonempty submask. Add the empty option separately if it is legal. Enumerating submasks for every mask costs three to the power n across all masks.
  • Exact integer geometry
    Use dot and cross products for structural tests. For route costs use the distance definition required by the problem and a sufficiently wide type. Exact squared distance and Euclidean length may give different optima.
See the idea in contest problems
