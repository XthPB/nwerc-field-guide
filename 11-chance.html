Probability, expectations and games

Flip two independent coins. The first is fair and the second has a three-in-four chance of heads. What are the chances of zero, one and two heads? Each row in the diagram shows all the probability after one more flip.

Start this topic in reading order → · See the whole reading path

  • Follow probability through one trial at a timeTwo independent coins show heads with probabilities 1/2 and 3/4. Flip each once. Find the probability of seeing exactly one head, counting either order of heads and tails. With many coins you may need the complete distribution of head counts.
  • First-step analysis and self-loopsEach press of a button succeeds independently with probability 1/4. Keep pressing until the first success. Find the expected total number of presses, including the press that eventually succeeds.
  • Several mutually dependent expectationsYou start at A. A move from A always reaches B. At B, a fair coin sends you home or back to A. Home ends the trip. Find the expected number of moves before you arrive home, including moves on repeated visits.
  • When a random gcd can stay where it isStart with the number 4. On each turn draw uniformly from 1,2,3,4 with replacement and update your number to the gcd of it and the draw. Stop when it becomes 1. Find the expected number of draws still needed from the start.
  • Track only the moments a nonlinear reward readsIn a sequence of independent trials a success extends the current run and a failure resets it to zero. Your reward depends on the square of the current run length. Find its expected value. Two fair trials give a small distribution you can enumerate.
  • Change the objective by tracking its incremental expected rewardTrials succeed or fail independently with known probabilities. Every maximal consecutive run of successes of length L scores L². Find the expected total score of the entire sequence, including a run that continues through its final trial.
  • A random walk can have an acyclic direction and a cyclic rowA robot starts in a given grid cell and stops when it reaches the bottom row. Each step it chooses uniformly from staying put, going down, and going left or right where a neighboring cell exists. Find its expected number of steps before it stops.
  • Count interchangeable species and condition on a real changeThree species compete. Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper, and paper beats rock. Each turn a random pair meets and, if the species differ, the loser is removed. Given the starting populations, find the probability that each species eventually survives alone.
  • Choose before or after observing a random outcomeA coin shows heads or tails with equal probability. Guess correctly and earn 1, otherwise earn 0. Compare a game where you must commit before the toss with one where you can choose after seeing it. Find the best expected reward under each information rule.
  • Decide whether to keep a reward or try once moreA fair six-sided die pays the number showing. After the first roll you can accept it or reroll once, in which case you must accept the second result. Decide what to do for each first roll to maximize your expected payout.
  • Learn an unknown probability as you make decisionsA machine secretly chooses one failure probability uniformly between 0 and 1 and keeps that same probability for every trial. You see two failures in a row. Given those observations, what probability should you assign to a failure on the next trial?
  • Recover the most likely hidden state sequenceA machine is either healthy or faulty at each time step. You know its starting probabilities, the chances of switching state, and how likely each observed signal is in each state. Given a sequence of sensor readings, recover the single most likely sequence of hidden states.
  • Winning positions, minimax, and sums of independent gamesTwo players share two heaps of stones. On a turn a player removes one or two stones from exactly one heap. A player with no legal move loses. Given the starting sizes, decide who can force a win if both choose their moves well.
  • When one move splits a game in twoOn a turn a player chooses one pile and splits it into two unequal, nonempty piles. All resulting piles stay in play, and a player unable to split any pile loses. Starting with a pile of five, determine whether the first player has a winning move.
  • Cycles in adversarial games need retrograde analysisYou’re given a directed graph of game positions. Players alternate moving along one outgoing edge. A player stuck with no move loses, and a play that continues forever is a draw. Classify each starting position, including a pair of positions that lead only to each other.

Test your recognition
An action succeeds with probability p and otherwise restarts from the same state. Why does memoized recursion fail?

The recurrence contains its own unknown. Isolate the self-loop and divide by the probability of leaving. If p=0, success is unreachable.

Two independent impartial games each have nonzero Grundy number 3. Who wins if a turn changes exactly one game?

The starting XOR is 3 XOR 3 = 0, a losing position under finite normal play. The fact that either component alone is winning does not answer the sum.
