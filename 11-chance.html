Probability, expectations and games

Move from one coin toss to a process that can revisit a state. Then ask when the player acts, what is observed and whether another player controls the next move.

  • Follow probability through one trial at a timeTwo independent coins have head probabilities one half and three quarters. What is the chance of exactly one head?
  • First-step analysis and self-loopsA button succeeds with probability one quarter per press. On average how many presses will you need?
  • Several mutually dependent expectationsFrom A you always move to B. From B a fair coin sends you home or back to A. What is the expected number of moves until home?
  • A random walk can have an acyclic direction and a cyclic rowA walker can move sideways within a row or down to an already solved row. How can you find its expected remaining time?
  • Track only the moments a nonlinear reward readsA reward involves the square of a random run length. Is its mean length enough to calculate the reward?
  • Change the objective by tracking its incremental expected rewardScore each run of successes by its squared length. How do you find the average score as trials arrive?
  • Count interchangeable species and condition on a real changeIn a population of three competing species, two animals are picked at random. How likely is each species to survive?
  • Choose before or after observing a random outcomeMust you guess a fair coin before the toss, or may you pick an answer after seeing it?
  • Decide whether to keep a reward or try once moreA fair six-sided die pays its face value. You may keep the first result or reroll once and must take the second result. What is this option worth?
  • Recover the most likely hidden state sequenceA sensor observes signals but hides whether a machine is healthy or faulty at each time. Which complete hidden history best explains the observations?
  • Winning positions, minimax, and sums of independent gamesTwo heaps allow removing one or two stones from exactly one heap per turn. Who can force the last move?
  • When one move splits a game in twoA player splits one pile into two unequal positive piles. Players alternate. Does splitting a pile of five give a winning move?
  • Cycles in adversarial games need retrograde analysisTwo game positions lead only to each other. Does either player have a forced win?

Test your recognition
An action succeeds with probability p and otherwise restarts from the same state. Why does memoized recursion fail?

The recurrence contains its own unknown. Isolate the self-loop and divide by the probability of leaving. If p=0, success is unreachable.

Two independent impartial games each have nonzero Grundy number 3. Who wins if a turn changes exactly one game?

The starting XOR is 3 XOR 3 = 0, a losing position under finite normal play. The fact that either component alone is winning does not answer the sum.
