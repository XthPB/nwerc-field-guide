Dynamic programming on graphs

Find a cheapest route through a road network when you can make one road free. Arriving at a junction with the coupon unused gives you different onward fares from arriving after spending it. What needs to be written alongside the junction?

Start this topic in reading order → · See the whole reading path

  • Use shortest distances to expose a DAG of optimal pathsYou’re given a graph with positive edge lengths and two vertices s and t. A route’s length is the sum of its edge lengths. Count shortest routes from s to t and determine how many of those pass through a specified vertex.
  • Fix the number of moves to make a cyclic graph acyclic in timeIn a directed graph you start at s and must use exactly two edges, ending back at s. The edge s → a costs 2 and a → s costs −3. Vertices may be revisited. Find the cheapest walk satisfying the exact move count.
  • Index a shortest-path DP by the intermediates it allowsYou’re given the direct travel prices between cities. You gradually permit additional cities as transfer stops. After each addition, find the cheapest route for each pair of endpoints using only permitted intermediate cities. The endpoints themselves need not be on the permitted list.
  • Expand a graph by the small missing stateYou’re given a road network with nonnegative fares and one coupon that makes any single road free. Find the cheapest trip from your source to your destination. On a route costing 4 followed by 9, you may decide which of those fares to waive.
  • A pairwise edge price needs a pending first edgeA traveler pays for consecutive edges in pairs. Traversing weights a and b together costs (a+b)². Starting at a given vertex, find the minimum fare to each destination using complete pairs. A route with one unpaid edge still needs a partner.
  • Track visit parity even when a walk revisits verticesYou may walk along the edges of a small graph and revisit vertices. The request specifies, for every vertex, whether its number of visits must be odd or even. Find a walk using the fewest visited positions that realizes that pattern, counting its starting position as a visit.
  • Solve cycles locally and connect the componentsYou travel through a directed network and may revisit vertices. In one version each vertex offers a reward you may collect only once, and you want the greatest collectible reward. What can you safely treat as one place when the network lets you go back and forth between several vertices?
  • Strip trees, then close each cycleEach vertex has a weight and points to exactly one successor. Choose vertices of maximum total weight subject to the rule that the endpoints of an arrow cannot both be chosen. Chains may feed into a directed cycle, so choices at the two ends of the cycle must agree.
  • Compose a score while jumping through a functional graphEvery station has exactly one outgoing connection, and taking it earns a known reward. A query gives a starting station and a jump count k, which can be very large. Return the final station and total reward across exactly those k jumps. Connections can form cycles.
  • A transitive closure can make the entire SCC collectibleIn a directed graph you may jump from u to v whenever v is reachable from u. Construct a route visiting distinct vertices. First maximize the number visited, then minimize their total vertex weight among routes of that length. A strongly connected group lets you choose the order in which you collect its vertices.
  • Few terminals, many ordinary graph verticesIn a weighted undirected graph, a small set of vertices must all be connected. You may use other vertices and pay for each chosen edge only once, even when several routes share it. Find the minimum total edge cost of a network connecting every required vertex.

Test your recognition
There are 100000 ordinary graph vertices but seven required terminals. Which set belongs in the exponential coordinate?

Mask the seven terminals. Keep a graph vertex as the meeting point. Merge complementary terminal masks there and run shortest-path closure for the merged mask.

The two cheapest edges incident to a vertex have the same currently paid cost but different pending weights. Can you merge them?

Only if every possible partner edge induces the same continuation costs or a valid dominance rule applies. Retain the pending weight for a general pair price.
