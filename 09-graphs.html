Dynamic programming on graphs

When two routes meet at the same vertex, what past information can still change the price of the next edge?

  • Expand a graph by the small missing stateA route has edges costing 4 and 9. One coupon makes one edge free. How do you find the cheapest trip?
  • Solve cycles locally and connect the componentsA directed route may enter a cycle at a and leave at b. Can the entire cycle be represented by one reward?
  • Strip trees, then close each cycleEach vertex points to exactly one successor. Select maximum-weight vertices while never selecting adjacent endpoints of an edge.
  • Few terminals, many ordinary graph verticesConnect a few required terminals using a cheap shared network of edges. Why can three separate shortest paths cost too much?
  • Use shortest distances to expose a DAG of optimal pathsCount shortest routes and find how many pass through one chosen vertex.
  • Fix the number of moves to make a cyclic graph acyclic in timeFind the cheapest walk of exactly two edges when a cycle contains a negative edge.
  • A pairwise edge price needs a pending first edgeEdges are charged in pairs by squaring the sum of their weights. What should you remember halfway through a pair?
  • A transitive closure can make the entire SCC collectibleA route can jump between any mutually reachable vertices. How do you collect a component's worth of vertices before leaving?

Test your recognition
There are 100000 ordinary graph vertices but seven required terminals. Which set belongs in the exponential coordinate?

Mask the seven terminals. Keep a graph vertex as the meeting point. Merge complementary terminal masks there and run shortest-path closure for the merged mask.

The two cheapest edges incident to a vertex have the same currently paid cost but different pending weights. Can you merge them?

Only if every possible partner edge induces the same continuation costs or a valid dominance rule applies. Retain the pending weight for a general pair price.
