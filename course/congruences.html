Join clocks whose periods share factors

One bell rings at times 2 mod 6 and another at 5 mod 9. At what times do both ring? Multiplying periods blindly gives an incorrect cycle.

Work through the example

  1. Write time as 2+6k. The other bell requires 6k≡3 mod 9. The gcd of 6 and 9 is 3 and divides the difference.
  2. Divide by 3 to get 2k≡1 mod 3. Inverting 2 gives k≡2 mod 3, hence time≡14 mod 18.
  3. Restrict this arithmetic progression to the query interval using signed floor and ceiling division.

Why it works

To join residues a mod m and b mod n, gcd(m,n) must divide b−a. After dividing the congruence by that gcd, extended Euclid supplies the modular inverse. The merged period is lcm(m,n).

x≡a(modm),x≡b(modn)⟹gcd⁡(m,n)∣(b−a)x\equiv a\pmod m,\quad x\equiv b\pmod n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gcd(m,n)\mid(b-a)x≡a(modm),x≡b(modn)⟹gcd(m,n)∣(b−a)

Divisibility of the difference by this gcd is also sufficient for a joint solution.

When the conditions change

O(log min(m,n)) for one merge. Normalize negative residues and widen before multiplying. In modular exponent towers distinguish coprime bases from noncoprime cases. CSES Exponentiation II uses a prime modulus and needs care if the base is a multiple of it.

Try one change

Would 2 mod 6 and 4 mod 9 meet?

Show the reasoning

No. Their residues disagree modulo 3.
