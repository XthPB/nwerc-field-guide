Strings and pattern matching · 3 of 11

Compare many substrings without assuming hash equality is proof

You need thousands of equality or overlap checks between substrings of one fixed text. Repeated character-by-character comparison can dominate the algorithm.

Work through the example

  1. Let H[i+1]=H[i]B+code(s[i]) in one chosen modulus. For [l,r), subtract H[l]Bʳ⁻ˡ from H[r].
  2. Both strings now start at the same power, so equal strings of equal length have equal fingerprints.
  3. For an exact final decision, compare candidates explicitly when affordable, or use suffix-array LCP, KMP, or a trie with an appropriate query state.

Why it works

The normalized polynomial is independent of its original offset. Matching fingerprints are evidence with a collision possibility. In CF 514C a trie can spend one mismatch while descending; hashes can instead evaluate single-letter edits.

When the conditions change

O(n) preprocessing, O(1) per fixed-length fingerprint. Double hashing can lower collision risk under stated randomness assumptions. For adversarial deterministic inputs prefer an exact algorithm when available.
