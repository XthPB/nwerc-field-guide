Probability, expectation and randomization · 10 of 15

Measure expected visits from a flow across each cut

A random walk starts at s on a tree and stops on first visiting t. How many times, on average, is each vertex visited? A full system of equations is possible but the tree exposes a simpler invariant.

Work through the example

  1. For the chain s–a–t, let F(v) be expected departures from v divided by degree(v), and put F(t)=0. It is the expected number of traversals of any one outgoing edge from v.
  2. Net crossings toward t across each path edge are exactly one on every completed walk. Thus F(a)=1 and F(s)=2. The expected departure counts are 2 for both s and a.
  3. Add a leaf b at a. Its edge has zero net crossings, so F(b)=F(a)=1. The extra branch raises the expected visits at a to degree(a)×1=3.

Why it works

Every off-path edge separates a component containing neither exactly one of s,t, hence its two directional crossing counts agree in expectation. Its endpoints have equal F. On the s–t path F rises by one per edge moving away from t. For nonterminal vertices expected visits equal departures; count the terminal visit once.

When the conditions change

Two tree traversals suffice in O(n). Use the transition probabilities explicitly for a weighted random walk; the equal-conductance formula is specific to uniform neighbors. If s=t, only the starting visit occurs.

Try one change

A long side branch attaches at distance two from t along the main path. What F value reaches its farthest leaf?

Show the reasoning

Two. Every side edge carries zero net flow, so F stays constant along the branch.
