Turn square products into short cycles of prime factors

Select as few array entries as possible so that their product is a perfect square. The selection must be nonempty. Each positive input has at most seven positive divisors, which restricts how many distinct primes can remain after square factors are removed.

Work through the example

  1. Strip even prime exponents. Numbers 6,15,10 become edges 2–3, 3–5 and 2–5. Picking all three gives each prime degree two, so the product is a square.
  2. Represent a single remaining prime p by edge 1–p. A square alone answers 1 and two equal kernels answer 2. Otherwise build a simple undirected graph and look for its shortest cycle.
  3. Run breadth-first search from 1 and each occurring prime up to √A, where A is the largest input. A non-parent edge reached from u and v gives the usual candidate dist[u]+dist[v]+1. Take the least across roots.

Why it works

An even-degree edge set decomposes into cycles. The dummy vertex 1 also has even degree when every prime does, by the handshake lemma. On every edge representing two primes the smaller prime is at most √A, so every cycle contains an eligible root. A search rooted on a shortest cycle recovers its length.

When the conditions change

Eight divisors would already allow three distinct primes. Check the bound before modeling a number by one edge. With R eligible roots, searches cost O(R(V+E)) after factorization. Count parallel edges before deduplicating.

Try one change

Why not just XOR the prime labels as machine integers?

Show the reasoning

Different prime sets can have the same integer XOR. The state represents the parity of each prime independently.

From idea to code

Use smallest prime factors to strip even exponents. In the BFS store the parent edge and distance so a tree edge does not falsely close a cycle.
