Separate the ability to win a round from the ability to lose it

You have a dictionary and several rounds of a game. Players alternately append one letter, keeping the current word a prefix of some dictionary entry. A player with no move loses that round and starts the next one. Only the last round decides the match. Determine who can force that final win.

Work through the example

  1. Build the prefix trie. At a leaf the player to move loses under the usual goal and has already achieved the opposite goal of losing. Record two flags, W=false and L=true.
  2. At an internal node each flag is true if some child has that same flag false. Compute W and L separately from leaves to root. With just the word a, the root has W=true and L=false. Every round starter wins, so the original starter wins the match exactly when the number of rounds is odd.
  3. If W is false, the original starter can be made to lose every round and start again. If W and L are both true, the starter can intentionally lose earlier rounds to retain the opening move, then force a win in the last round.

Why it works

The opposite-goal flag describes a separate zero-sum game where the player seeks to be the one who runs out of moves. When W is true but L is false, the opponent can prevent the starter from losing intentionally. Applying the two flags to the remaining number of rounds gives the parity case.

When the conditions change

The terminal condition is having no legal child. A trie node that spells an entire dictionary word may still have children. Time is O(total input length) for a fixed alphabet and the number of rounds can be huge.
