Find common area by clipping one polygon one edge at a time

Two convex rectangles share a center, but one is rotated relative to the other. You know their corners or can compute them from their sizes and angles. Find the area that belongs to both rectangles, treating touching along an edge as zero area.

Work through the example

  1. Start with the four corners of the first rectangle in boundary order. A directed edge of the second counterclockwise rectangle keeps only the points on its left, including the boundary.
  2. Walk around the current polygon. For each segment from P to Q, append its crossing point when it passes between inside and outside. Append Q whenever Q is inside. For a square clipped by x≤0, this leaves its left half.
  3. Repeat for the other three edges. Apply the shoelace formula to the remaining vertices. For example two centered 4-by-2 rectangles at right angles overlap in a 2-by-2 square of area 4.

Why it works

A convex polygon is the intersection of the inner half-planes of its edges. After each pass the surviving polygon is exactly the intersection with all half-planes processed so far. A segment crosses a half-plane boundary at most once unless it lies on the boundary.

t=side⁡(P)side⁡(P)−side⁡(Q),X=P+t(Q−P)t=\frac{\operatorname{side}(P)}{\operatorname{side}(P)-\operatorname{side}(Q)},\qquad X=P+t(Q-P)t=side(P)−side(Q)side(P)​,X=P+t(Q−P)

The signed side is the oriented cross product with the directed clipping edge. Apply this formula for a genuine crossing.

When the conditions change

When signed side values a,b are strictly opposite, the intersection parameter on P→Q is a/(a−b). Handle boundary endpoints consistently and tolerate duplicate vertices. Two rectangles have constant total work. A nonconvex clipping shape needs another decomposition.
