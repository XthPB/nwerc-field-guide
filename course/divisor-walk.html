Probability, expectation and randomization · 4 of 15

Split a random divisor process into prime-exponent chains

A number becomes a uniformly chosen positive divisor of itself each round. After many rounds, what is its expected value? For a large starting number even listing all divisor sequences is expensive.

Work through the example

  1. Start from 12=2²·3. A uniform divisor independently chooses an exponent from {0,1,2} for 2 and {0,1} for 3. After one round, their expected powers are 7/3 and 2. The mean divisor is 14/3.
  2. For a fixed prime, store F[e], the probability that its exponent is e. From e it moves uniformly to each j from 0 through e. Sum F[e]/(e+1) over e≥j to get the new probability at j. A descending suffix sum computes the whole row.
  3. After the last round, sum F[e]pᵉ for each prime. Multiply these expectations, because the exponent chains use independent random choices at every step.

Why it works

The divisors of a factored number correspond one-to-one to tuples of exponents. The uniform distribution on this Cartesian product factors, and the next transition of any exponent depends only on its current value.

When the conditions change

After factorization, k rounds cost O(k·Σ eᵢ) arithmetic operations for initial exponents eᵢ. Exact fractions, floating point or modular inverses depend on the requested output. Choosing a proper divisor would couple the primes by excluding one joint outcome.

Try one change

What is the expectation after a step from a prime p?

Show the reasoning

Its divisors are 1 and p, equally likely, so the expectation is (1+p)/2.
