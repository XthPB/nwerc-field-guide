Newer Codeforces ideas

Each vertex of a directed graph carries a positive integer. Count paths of at least two vertices in which every later label equals the sum of the previous two. In the diagram, follow labels 1, 2, 3 and 5 and ask when a new edge can extend an old path.

  • Represent a convex tree DP by its sorted marginal costsEach child in a tree tells you the minimum cost of supplying 0, 1, 2 or more units. You need exactly k units in total and may distribute the request among the children. Find the cheapest split when each child’s successive extra units have nondecreasing costs.
  • Undo an invertible decision chainA point starts at 0 on a line and ends at 0 after a sequence of reflections. Reflecting x around center c sends it to 2c−x. The first center is missing and the remaining centers are 4, 1 and 3 in that order. Recover the missing center.
  • Quotient rounding can leave only two states per depthStart with a pile of 19. A split replaces a pile by two piles as equal in size as possible, so an odd pile yields two sizes differing by one. Determine whether any descendant pile can have size 5, and how many splits along its branch are needed.
  • A cap on the maximum becomes bounded denomination stockDivide a total into positive integer group sizes. A size s used c times incurs a penalty of s·c, and the score is the largest such penalty. Minimize that score. For a total of 8, first ask which group quantities would be allowed if every penalty were capped at 3.
  • Compress edge-pair transitions by the previous labelVertices in a directed graph have positive integer labels. Count paths with at least two vertices in which every label after the first two equals the sum of the preceding two. A chain labeled 1,2,3,5 allows several valid starts and finishes.
  • A predecessor must become eligible before a range maximum can see itYou have positions on a line. Each has a reward and a minimum separation from any other chosen position. Select a maximum-reward set such that the gap between two chosen positions meets both of their requirements. An earlier choice may become eligible only after you move far enough right.
  • One family of intervals contributes a ramp at every covered indexYou have a digit string. Call a substring bad if you can read 2025 in order inside it but cannot read 2026 in order. For each position, count the substrings covering it that are good. The example shortens the two test patterns to ab and ac so you can check the count by hand.
  • Only four witnesses determine a geometric scoreEach point on a map has a reward for removing it. Keep at least one point. Your score is the rewards for the removed points plus the perimeter of the smallest axis-aligned rectangle enclosing the points you kept. Which points should you keep to get the highest score?
  • Exact nearest-point distances turn into clauses on adjacent surviving sitesTreasures occupy some sites along a line. A clue at a site tells you its exact distance to the nearest treasure. Count placements satisfying every clue. If site 3 on a five-site line reports distance 2, at least one of sites 1 and 5 must hold a treasure.
  • Summarize a deterministic subtree traversal that resets its own memoryA walker starts inside a full binary tree with an exit just above its top vertex. At a fresh internal vertex it first goes left, then right on returning, then up on returning again. Leaves immediately send it up. With all markers initially clear, how many edge crossings does each possible starting vertex need to reach the exit?
  • Turn “all pairs are compatible” into a chain through each objectYou’re given several curves on the same domain. Select as many as possible so they can be stacked in one consistent vertical order across the entire domain. A pair that changes order by crossing is incompatible even if it looks correctly ordered at the endpoints.
  • A stochastic process can reduce to choosing a deterministic orderSome vertices of a tree are red and the rest are black. Each turn you choose a vertex, which copies the color of a uniformly random neighbor. Starting with at least one red vertex, choose which vertices to try so that the expected number of turns until the entire tree is red is as small as possible.
  • Count strings that contain every allowed subsequenceA target word is chosen one stage at a time, with a set of allowed letters at each stage. Count candidate words of the requested length that contain every possible target as a subsequence. For stages {a,b} then {c}, a candidate must contain both ac and bc in order.
  • Expand a square and factor the coefficient of a pairA grid has a number in each cell. For every axis-aligned subrectangle, add its entries, square that sum, and contribute the square to the answer. Find the total over all subrectangles. A 1 × 2 row [2,3] already gives three different rectangles to count.
  • Classify vertices by the complete paths that contain themIn a directed grid with no cycles, consider every complete path from the specified start to the finish. Group cells according to which complete paths contain them. Two cells belong together only if every complete path visits both of them or visits neither.
  • Move a tree-cut DP into the XOR span of allowed totalsEach tree vertex has a binary label. You may cut edges, splitting the tree into components. Count the choices of cut edges for which the XOR of all labels in each component belongs to a given allowed set. Every component must pass its own test.
  • Build a long DP row by reusing slicesYou arrive with zero dollars on a day of your choice. On a day priced at a dollars, you spend a if you can afford it. Otherwise you work and earn a. For every possible arrival day, find the money you have after the final day. Start with daily prices 2 and 1.

Grow Fibonacci paths one edge at a time

1 / 3

Edge 1→2

No predecessor is available. One two-vertex path ends here.

Edge 2→3

The required preceding value is 3−2=1. Extend the earlier path or start fresh, giving 2.

Edge 3→5

The required label is 5−3=2. Both previous paths can extend. The new edge has 3 ways, so all edges together account for 6.

Test your recognition
In a cut-tree problem, weights have 30 bits but every completed component must equal one of six listed XORs. What bounds your state count?

Completed-component XORs belong to the binary span of the six allowed values, of dimension at most six. Track closed XOR in that basis; the open XOR follows from the fixed subtree XOR.

Two cells have the same number of complete paths passing through them. May they share a universal co-occurrence class?

Equality of counts is insufficient. In a diamond the two branch cells each see one path, but the paths differ. Test mutual dominance and postdominance or an equivalent exact path-set criterion.
