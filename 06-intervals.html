Interval and partition DP

You have four piles in a line, of sizes 3, 1, 2 and 4. Merge neighboring piles until one remains, paying their combined size at every merge. Which merge order gives the smallest total bill?

Start this topic in reading order → · See the whole reading path

  • Exactly k consecutive piecesYou’re given the array 1, 2, 1. Cut it into exactly two consecutive nonempty pieces, so each element belongs to one piece. Each piece costs the square of its sum. Choose the cut that minimizes the combined cost.
  • Know when a segment contains every copy of its valuesIn the array 4, 1, 4, 2, 1, 3 you may select disjoint contiguous segments. A segment is legal only if it contains every occurrence in the array of each value it includes. Starting at the first 4, find where a legal segment can end before scoring it.
  • Sort the values and undo the last extremeInsert 1, 4, 5 and 10 one at a time in an order you choose. After each insertion pay the largest inserted value minus the smallest inserted value. Minimize the total payment. In order 4,5,1,10 the payments are 0, 1, 4 and 9.
  • Split on the final merge, pivot, or trianglePiles of weights 3, 1 and 2 stand in a row. A move replaces two neighboring piles by one pile whose weight is their sum. You pay that sum on every move. Find the smallest total payment needed to leave one pile.
  • Choose a matrix parenthesization by the last multiplicationYou must multiply three matrices in their given order. Their dimensions are 10 × 30, 30 × 5 and 5 × 60. You may choose which adjacent pair to multiply first. Find the grouping using the fewest scalar multiplications.
  • Two ends, score difference, and forced turnsThe numbers 8, 15, 3 and 7 lie in a row. Two players take turns removing either end and adding it to their own score until the row is empty. Both play optimally. How many points can the first player guarantee?
  • Let matching endpoints join a palindrome deletionYou’re given a sequence of letters. In one move you may erase any contiguous block that reads the same in both directions. The remaining pieces then touch. Find the fewest moves that erase A, B, C, A.
  • Delete, paint, or merge matching endpointsA string shrinks as you erase letters. One move erases a contiguous run of equal letters, after which the remaining sides join. Find the fewest moves that erase aba. Initially its two occurrences of a are separated.
  • Keep the group that will merge with an interval boundaryA row initially reads a,b,a,a. A move removes a contiguous block of equal colors and pays the square of the number removed. The remaining blocks slide together. Choose moves that maximize the total score, possibly saving matching colors for a later, larger removal.
  • Noncrossing pairs, parsing, and interleavingPlace four ordered points on a line. Pair all points and draw one arc above the line for each pair. Arcs may nest but may never cross. Count the possible complete pairings. Pairing the first point creates separate obligations inside and outside its arc.
  • Count deletion orders by interleaving independent regionsIn one move delete two adjacent equal letters and close the gap. Starting with aaaa, count different complete move sequences that erase the entire string. Two sequences differ if they remove different original position pairs or remove the pairs in a different order.
  • Cut a circular instance at a justified boundaryPile weights lie around a circular necklace. You may merge neighboring piles, paying their combined weight, until only one remains. Find the minimum total cost. In the circle 5,1,4 the piles 4 and 5 are neighbors even though they sit at opposite ends of the written list.

Test your recognition
A reward depends quadratically on how many equal-coloured blocks are removed together. Can the unit-cost deletion recurrence be reused?

Matching a distant block changes the eventual group size and thus its reward. Give the interval a third coordinate for attached equal blocks before attempting any speedup.

A problem counts feasible deletion orders. Fixing the final pivot leaves left and right independent. Is multiplying the two counts sufficient?

Only if the relative schedule between sides is fixed. If L and R moves can interleave arbitrarily, multiply by choose(L+R,L) as well.
